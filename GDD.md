# Scrapdrift

Game Design Document & Overview

## High-Concept Pitch

Scrapdrift is a bite-sized, post-apocalyptic racing roguelike rendered as chunky top-down pixel art. Players pilot scavenged vehicles through violently decaying, procedurally generated tracks where a single collision means instant death. Surviving rewards you with scrap metal to buy incremental upgrades before being thrown back into the mechanical grinder.

## Core Gameplay Loop

### The Race

You start at the back of the pack on a newly generated loop. You must balance high speed with absolute precision, utilizing a snappy arcade drift to survive tight corners.

### The Hazard Escalation

Every 3 laps, the track regenerates into a longer track. After a few tracks, obstacles appear: debris falls, oil slicks bloom on apexes, and hostile barriers spawn, forcing you to constantly adapt your racing line.

### The Instant Failure

There are no health bars for impacts. Hitting a wall or an obstacle destroys your vehicle instantly, ending your run.

### The Upgrade Hub

Your final finishing position determines how much scrap metal you salvage. You spend this scrap on permanent stats and modular perks to push further on the next run. There isn't a separate place for this. You do it at the race line: it's just UI that appears, and clicking it instantly starts the next lap.

## Art & Audio Direction

### Visual Style

Hand-drawn 2D pixel art, viewed from a fixed top-down camera that frames the entire circuit at once. The whole game renders at a low internal resolution and scales up with nearest-neighbour filtering, so pixels stay large, square and honest. No smoothing, no sub-pixel drift, no rotated art that turns to mush.

The reference points are 90s arcade and 16-bit top-down racers: Micro Machines, Super Off Road, Death Rally, Neo Drift Out. Small readable vehicles, heavy black outlines, high contrast against the track surface. Since a wall tap ends the run, readability beats detail everywhere the two conflict.

### Palette

A tight, restricted palette holds the whole game together. Roughly 24 to 32 colours total, split into three roles:

- **Track and terrain:** desaturated greys, rust browns, oil-stained near-blacks. This is the bulk of the screen and it should stay quiet.
- **Vehicles:** saturated, clearly distinct hues so you never lose your own car in a pack.
- **Hazard and UI:** a handful of hot accents, yellow-and-black warning stripes, hazard orange, sickly green. Reserved exclusively for things that can kill you or things you can collect. Nothing decorative is ever allowed to use these.

That last rule does the heavy lifting. If a colour is loud, it means something.

### Vehicle Sprites

The car rotates freely, which is the one real technical decision in the art pipeline. Two options:

- **Pre-rendered rotation frames.** Draw the car at 16, 32 or 64 fixed angles and snap to the nearest. Authentic to the era, every frame stays crisp, and the visible snapping actually reads as retro rather than broken. More art per vehicle.
- **Free rotation of a single sprite.** One sprite, rotated smoothly. Far less work, but rotated pixel art shimmers and breaks up unless the sprite is drawn quite large.

Pre-rendered frames at 32 angles is the recommended default. It suits the drift, since the nose swinging out through discrete steps looks deliberate.

Drift state gets read at a glance through the sprite: tyre smoke puffs, skid decals left on the asphalt, and a slight sprite offset as the tail steps out.

### The Environment

A grim industrial racetrack seen from above. Cracked asphalt with oil stains and tyre scars, grey concrete barriers with chipped edges, chain-link and rebar in the run-off. Warning-stripe accents mark every hazardous zone.

Detail lives in the surface, not in clutter. Anything that reads as an obstacle must actually be one, because a player who brakes for decoration loses a run they should have won.

### Screen Treatment

A subtle CRT pass over the final image: light scanlines, a touch of chromatic bleed on bright pixels, very slight vignette. It should be felt more than seen. Any effect strong enough to obscure a barrier edge is turned down until it isn't.

## Procedural Track Generation

### Chunk-Based Assembly

Tracks are generated by snapping together pre-drawn 2D "chunks" (straights, chicanes, hairpins) like puzzle pieces. Each chunk is a fixed-size tile authored on the pixel grid, with entry and exit openings that line up so seams never show.

### Infinite Variety

A lightweight script handles the stitching logic, ensuring every run creates a unique, closed-loop circuit while maintaining strict playability and clear racing lines.

## Metaprogression & Upgrades

Scrap is earned relative to your placement. First place yields massive rewards; a fiery first-lap crash yields a tiny handful of scrap.

Example upgrades under consideration:

- **Engine Tuning:** Higher top speed and faster acceleration, at the cost of harder handling.
- **Reinforced Chassis (The Bumper):** Allows you to survive exactly one glancing wall tap per run before breaking.
- **Tire Compounds:** Tighter drift control and faster cornering recovery.
- **Scrap Magnet:** Multiplier that increases the amount of currency scavenged from near-misses and high finishes.
